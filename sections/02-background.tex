\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Expert-Parallel Communication}
\label{sec:epbackground}
An MoE layer selects up to $\topk$ experts for each input token. Under expert parallelism, experts are distributed across GPU ranks. Dispatch moves activations to the selected expert ranks; after expert computation, Combine returns and merges their contributions at the token owner~\cite{deepep,ncclep}.

Routing determines the amount of traffic sent to each destination. Communication therefore involves both activations or partial outputs and metadata describing destinations, valid counts, and output placement. Within a node, ranks may use NVLink; across nodes, they use a network transport such as RDMA. Communication staging provides known landing addresses, while the final expert layout may depend on exchanged counts.

Three events must remain distinct. Receive resources must be writable before remote delivery. Required input must be complete and visible before a consumer reads it. A buffer becomes reusable only after its last consumer finishes. A notification that establishes data arrival does not necessarily establish safe buffer reuse.

\subsection{Synchronization Overhead in Small-Batch EP}
\label{sec:epsynchronization}
Cross-node DeepEP V2 Direct places coordination at both ends of Dispatch and Combine~\cite{deepepcode}. An entry barrier precedes payload injection. Dispatch exchanges counts and computes layout alongside data movement; Combine reuses the established routing. After transfer, asynchronous-write waits, local joins, and GIN flush/fence operations precede an EP-wide completion-signal exchange.

Direct already reserves communication buffers; changing this schedule must preserve their lifetime and data-visibility requirements. We focus on cross-node Direct, since Hybrid, LL, and NCCL EP HT use different control structures~\cite{deepepcode,ncclllcode,ncclhtcode}.

At fixed EP size, fewer tokens reduce payload work while coordination still involves the same ranks. Our EP8 measurements show entry synchronization near 15\us{}, occupying about 20\% of the operator at eight tokens per rank and 5\% at 128. This motivates overlap, although the measured interval is not entirely removable. Section~\ref{sec:evidence} presents the methodology and results.

\subsection{Opportunities for In-Network Computing}
\label{sec:incopportunity}
In-network computing (INC) supports stateful processing and aggregation along the data path, as used in collective reduction and MoE communication~\cite{sharp,switchml,dysharp}. We explore its use for synchronization, requiring bounded programmable storage and ordered notifications.

At entry, a locally ready rank can upload into reserved network storage while INC aggregates readiness announcements; destination delivery still waits for the required readiness and writable resources. At completion, INC can track expected output and append an ordered notification to the destination's final data. The notification must imply data visibility, and genuine global dependencies remain.

\paragraph{Key insight.}
Synchronization conditions must hold, but their communication can overlap data transfer: aggregate readiness during upload and deliver completion notifications with trailing data. This reduces exposed coordination time when safe staging and ordered completion are available.
